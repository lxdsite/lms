01 · Mechanism

Defining Common Vision & Identity

Mutual vs. Individual Value Fairness in Appropriation

The most foundational mechanism — and the most often skipped — is establishing a shared answer to "who are we together?" A common ecosystem identity built on shared values and culture creates a basis for cooperation that doesn't depend entirely on contracts or monitoring.

When actors see themselves as part of a collective with common purpose, purely individual value-maximising behaviour is tempered by a sense of membership. Norms emerge — unwritten but understood — about what is acceptable and what would damage the group. This matters especially for fairness tensions: actors tolerate an imperfect value distribution more readily when they believe others are acting in good faith.

Common Values & Culture

Shared commitments — to quality, openness, or customer outcomes — create a reference point for decisions. These values function as soft governance, guiding behaviour where formal rules are silent.

Collective Identity

Beyond values, actors need a sense of belonging. Identity creates loyalty and reciprocal commitment that outlasts any single transaction, making ecosystems more resilient when tensions flare.

In Practice

Linux and open-source communities sustain contribution from thousands of actors — many competitors — through a shared identity built around open knowledge and collective progress. No single actor "wins"; the identity of the ecosystem is the draw.

02 · Mechanism

Building Trust, Reciprocity & Concurrency

Value Creation vs. Capture Mutual vs. Individual Value Fairness in Appropriation

Trust is the lubricant of ecosystem collaboration. Without it, every interaction becomes a negotiation. Ecosystems that sustain cooperation build multiple layers of trust, reinforced by patterns of reciprocal exchange and simultaneous co-development.

Functional Trust
Confidence that partners reliably deliver on technical and operational commitments. Built through track record and competence signals.
Proxy Trust
Trust extended via endorsement by a credible third party — certifications, referrals, platform membership, or shared affiliations.
Reflective Trust
Trust from mutual understanding — knowing how a partner thinks and behaves under pressure. Develops through sustained interaction over time.
Institutional & Calculative Trust
Trust grounded in formal structures — contracts, standards, regulatory oversight — and rational assessment of incentives.
Reciprocity

The expectation of mutual exchange — balanced over time. When actors invest knowing others will invest in return, cooperation becomes self-reinforcing rather than fragile.

Concurrency

Simultaneous development among ecosystem actors rather than sequential handoffs. Concurrent evolution builds shared momentum and surfaces interdependencies early.

03 · Mechanism

Cultivating Dynamic & Interorganisational Capabilities

Value Creation vs. Capture Mutual vs. Individual Value

The ability to adapt — to absorb new actors, respond to shifts, and reconfigure around new opportunities — depends on capabilities that span organisational boundaries. These are system-level capabilities developed collectively, not just within individual firms.

Ecosystem Sensing
Opportunity Screening · Partnership Scanning

The collective ability to read the environment — identifying emerging opportunities, spotting threats, and screening partners. Requires open information flows and a culture of shared intelligence rather than hoarding.

Ecosystem Seizing
Value Proposition Development · Ecosystem Formation

The ability to act on what the ecosystem senses — developing new value propositions together and forming new partnerships to address an opportunity before competitors do. Speed and coordination matter as much as insight.

Ecosystem Reconfiguring
Adaptive Value Creation · Economic Resilience

The ability to restructure roles, relationships, and value flows when conditions change — without fragmenting the ecosystem. Requires flexibility in structure and resilience in relationships simultaneously.

In Practice

The semiconductor ecosystem — spanning chip designers, fabs, equipment makers, and software tooling — continuously reconfigures around new process nodes and application demands. No single firm holds all capabilities; ecosystem-wide sensing and seizing keeps the system viable through each technological generation.

04 · Mechanism

Flexible Alignment & Governance

Value Creation vs. Capture Mutual vs. Individual Value Fairness in Appropriation

Rigid governance structures stifle the adaptability that makes ecosystems valuable. Flexible alignment is the ability of diverse actors to coordinate without a fixed hierarchy or exhaustive rulebook — achieving coherence without uniformity.

Shared Purpose

A common direction guiding coordination without requiring agreement on everything. Purpose governs decisions where rules are silent.

Adaptability

Governance structures that evolve — revisable agreements, flexible roles, and processes that don't require consensus on every adjustment.

Interdependence with Autonomy

Actors are mutually dependent yet retain freedom within agreed boundaries — preserving innovation incentives while maintaining coherence.

Loose Coupling

Interfaces designed so changes in one part don't cascade through the whole. Modularity enables resilience and independent evolution.

In Practice

The internet's governance model — standards set by open bodies like IETF, with decentralised implementation by thousands of independent actors — exemplifies flexible alignment. No one controls the system, yet it coordinates reliably across billions of devices through shared protocols and a loose but durable governance architecture.

05 · Synthesis

Four Mechanisms, Three Tensions

No single mechanism resolves all ecosystem tensions on its own. They work in combination — each addressing different root causes, and reinforcing each other when applied together.

Common Vision & Identity

Creates the baseline commitment that makes the other mechanisms possible. Without shared purpose, trust is transactional, governance adversarial, and capabilities hoarded.

Trust, Reciprocity & Concurrency

Reduces the friction cost of cooperation and makes long-term investment feel safe. Without trust, actors default to protective behaviour that limits value creation for all.

Dynamic Capabilities

Ensures the ecosystem adapts without fragmenting. Without shared sensing and reconfiguring, ecosystems become rigid and are displaced by more agile competitors.

Flexible Governance

Provides structural conditions for coordination without control. Without governance, trust and identity alone cannot resolve disputes over value appropriation at scale.
