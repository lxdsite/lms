01   Dosi (1982)

Technological Paradigms & Trajectories

Giovanni Dosi argued that technological change is not random — it follows structured paths. A technological paradigm is a shared model or blueprint that defines what problems to solve and how to solve them (analogous to Kuhn's scientific paradigms). Think of it as the "rulebook" an industry unconsciously follows.

Within a paradigm, progress moves along a technological trajectory — the specific direction of improvement over time (e.g., faster chips, smaller devices). Most innovation is incremental, happening within the trajectory. Radical change only occurs when a paradigm is overthrown entirely.

This explains why dominant designs persist, why firms get locked in, and why breakthrough innovations are so disruptive — they don't just improve the product, they rewrite the rulebook.

⚠ Limitations

Dosi's framework is better at explaining past trajectories than predicting future ones. It underplays the role of demand, user behaviour, and policy in shaping technological paths. It can also imply technological determinism — as if markets have no choice but to follow a trajectory.

02   Bommel et al.

Learning Spillovers & Affordable Loss

Bommel et al. introduced the concept of learning spillovers — the idea that knowledge and insights generated in one context "spill over" to benefit adjacent areas, teams, or organisations, often unintentionally. Innovations rarely stay contained; learning leaks, and that leakage can accelerate broader adoption.

Linked to this is the principle of affordable loss: rather than projecting expected returns before acting, innovators should ask "how much am I willing to lose?" This reframes risk. It encourages experimentation because failure is bounded — you only risk what you can afford, not what you hope to gain.

Together, these ideas support a learn-by-doing culture: small, low-stakes trials that generate spillover insights are more generative than large, high-stakes bets.

⚠ Limitations

Affordable loss can be misread as an excuse for underinvestment. Spillovers also benefit competitors as much as collaborators, raising IP and competitive moat concerns. The framework works best in early-stage exploration, not at scale.

03   Bommel et al.

Easy-to-Difficult Trials

Bommel et al. advocate for sequencing innovation experiments from easy to difficult — starting with low-complexity, high-probability-of-success trials before escalating to harder challenges. This is not timidity; it's strategic momentum-building.

Early wins create confidence, organisational buy-in, and proof of concept. They also surface unexpected variables in a low-risk environment, so that by the time harder trials begin, the team is better informed and more resilient to failure.

This sequencing mirrors how learners build mastery — and applies equally to product pilots, R&D roadmaps, and change management in organisations adopting new technologies.

⚠ Limitations

The "easy first" logic can create a false sense of security — what's easy in a lab may be hard in a real market. There's also a risk of spending too long in the easy zone, delaying the critical learning that only comes from harder, messier trials.

04   Product Development

Overfeaturing in New Products

Overfeaturing occurs when products are loaded with more features than users need, want, or can navigate — driven by engineering ambition, competitive pressure, or misread market signals. The result is bloated products that confuse rather than delight.

The stage-gate view of overfeaturing identifies how each development phase becomes a battleground where new features are added but rarely removed. Without a ruthless prioritisation mechanism, every gate becomes an accumulation point rather than a filter.

Why It Happens

Fear of missing out on edge-case users, internal politics, and the "more = better" assumption.

The Cost

Higher cognitive load, slower adoption, increased development cost, and diluted core value.

The Fix

Jobs-to-be-done framing, MVP discipline, and user-led feature validation at each gate.

⚠ Limitations

Stage-gate models can be too linear for fast-moving markets. The "right" feature set is deeply context-dependent, and lean approaches risk under-building for sophisticated users.

05   IoT

IoT Paradigm Absorption

The Internet of Things (IoT) represents a full paradigm shift in Dosi's terms — not just a new product category, but a new rulebook for how objects, data, and people interact. IoT absorption describes how industries, organisations, and societies gradually internalise this paradigm into their operations and mental models.

Absorption is not instant. It moves through phases: awareness → experimentation → integration → dependence. Most organisations today sit somewhere between experimentation and integration, still grappling with interoperability, data governance, and skills gaps.

What makes IoT uniquely challenging is that its value is emergent — it only appears when devices, platforms, and people connect at scale. This makes early ROI hard to demonstrate, slowing adoption despite the technology's maturity.

⚠ Limitations

IoT adoption is hampered by fragmentation — incompatible standards, vendor lock-in, and security vulnerabilities. Measuring "absorption" is also difficult; surface-level adoption (connected devices) can mask shallow organisational integration.

06   Gartner

The Hype Cycle

Gartner's Hype Cycle is a visual model describing how technologies move from invention to mainstream adoption — and why the journey is rarely smooth. It maps the emotional and market journey of a technology through five stages.

1
Technology Trigger
2
Peak of Inflated Expectations
3
Trough of Disillusionment
4
Slope of Enlightenment
5
Plateau of Productivity

The Trough of Disillusionment mirrors the Valley of Dissolution — the danger zone where early hype crashes into real-world complexity. Survivors of this trough emerge with realistic, sustainable adoption. Those who can't wait, invest during the trough when assets are undervalued.

⚠ Limitations

The Hype Cycle is descriptive, not predictive — it can't tell you where a technology currently sits or how long each phase lasts. It also treats all technologies as following the same arc, ignoring how context, regulation, and competition alter the curve dramatically.

07   GenAI

GenAI as a Technological Paradigm

Generative AI is not merely a new tool — through Dosi's lens, it represents an emerging technological paradigm: a new model that redefines what is possible, what problems are worth solving, and how knowledge work is organised. Its trajectory is still being defined, but the direction is clear: from automation of tasks to augmentation of cognition.

Adoption trends mirror Rogers' diffusion curve. Innovators (researchers, startups) embraced GenAI early. Early Adopters (tech-forward enterprises) are now integrating it into workflows. The Early Majority — cautious, ROI-focused organisations — are beginning to cross in. Late Majority and Laggards remain sceptical, often citing hallucination risks, job displacement fears, and compliance uncertainty.

On the Hype Cycle, GenAI likely peaked around 2023–2024 and is now descending toward the Trough — where realistic value assessment will separate genuine transformation from overpromised potential.

⚠ Limitations

GenAI adoption is uneven across sectors and geographies. Hallucination, bias, and energy costs remain unresolved. The paradigm framing can overstate coherence — GenAI is a cluster of diverse technologies, not a single unified shift. Regulatory frameworks are still forming, creating adoption uncertainty.

08   Synthesis

Putting It All Together

These frameworks are not isolated theories — they form an interconnected lens for understanding how technologies emerge, spread, stall, and ultimately reshape industries.

How Tech Evolves

Dosi's paradigms and trajectories explain the structured, path-dependent nature of technological progress.

How Adoption Happens

Rogers, Gladwell, and the Hype Cycle map the social and emotional journey from invention to mainstream use.

How Organisations Learn

Bommel's affordable loss and easy-to-difficult trials offer practical strategies for navigating uncertainty.

Where It Can Go Wrong

Overfeaturing, the Valley of Dissolution, and the Trough of Disillusionment mark the failure points to navigate.

The most resilient innovators understand both the technical trajectory they're riding and the human adoption journey they must navigate. Neither is sufficient alone.
